# The goal, and why anyone should pay for it

A goal states an outcome someone will notice. Not a feature, not a project name, not a
technology.

| Not a goal | A goal |
| :-- | :-- |
| build the statement module | a customer can answer "what did I spend" without calling us |
| migrate to the new platform | releases stop needing a weekend, so we can fix things the week they are reported |
| add reporting | the operations team stops rebuilding the same spreadsheet every Monday |

The test: **could you tell, six months later, whether it happened?** If not, it is a plan
disguised as a goal, and everything prioritised against it will be prioritised by taste.

## Attach a measure, even a rough one

A goal without a measure cannot lose an argument with a louder goal. The measure does not have
to be precise - "calls about statements, currently about forty a week" is enough to notice a
change, and far better than "improved customer experience".

Where the measure genuinely cannot be taken, say what you would accept as evidence instead. That
sentence is the thing a team will use two months later when a trade-off appears.

## Say who it is for

A goal serving "users" serves nobody in particular and will be argued about forever. Name the
role: the customer, the operations officer, the compliance team, the relationship manager. When
two of them want opposite things - which happens constantly - you have discovered the real
decision, and it is yours to make rather than to average away.

## Value is comparative, not absolute

Everything on a list is valuable, which is why arguing about value in isolation goes nowhere.
The useful question is never "is this valuable" but **"is this more valuable than that, this
quarter, given what it costs"**.

That question needs a rough cost, which is why a goal without any sense of effort cannot be
prioritised honestly. Ask the BA and the PM for a rough size before deciding order - not a
precise one, which does not exist yet.

## Client work

The goals are usually the customer's, stated in their words, and your job is to make them
checkable rather than to invent them. Push back on one that cannot be told apart from its
opposite six months later - politely, early, and in writing - because at the end of the project
somebody will ask whether it was achieved.

Where the customer's stated goal and their evident behaviour disagree, the behaviour is the
truth. Write down what they asked for, and raise the discrepancy rather than quietly building to
what you think they meant.
